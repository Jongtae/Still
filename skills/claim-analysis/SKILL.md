# Analysing claims and opinions

Use this when the owner wants material judged: whether a claim holds, how convincing an argument or a review is, or which of several views is better supported. It works the same for any subject.

Do not use it when the owner only asked what the material says. Then summarise it and keep the author's claims as the author's.

## 1. Pull out the claims

- List the claims that matter for the owner's question, in the material's own terms. Keep a claim of fact ("sales fell 10% in 2025") apart from a judgment ("the strategy failed") and from a prediction or recommendation.
- Note who makes each claim and what stake they may have in it, when the material shows it.
- Leave out claims that do not bear on the question. Say so if you leave out many.

## 2. Find the support for each claim

For each claim, note what the material offers for it: data with a source, a named study or document, an example, an expert's opinion, or nothing.

- Check a claim of fact against a source other than the one making it when the answer turns on it. Use the `source-cross-check` method when it is installed.
- An example is not proof of a general claim. An opinion, even an expert's, is support of a different kind than data; say which kind it is.

## 3. Look for counter-evidence and disagreement

- Look for evidence against each important claim, not only for it: a contrary finding, a different dataset, a known limitation.
- When informed people disagree, say so and give the strongest version of each side. Do not settle a real disagreement by choosing the view that sounds more confident.
- Point out reasoning gaps you can see: a conclusion that goes further than its evidence, a cause read from a correlation, a missing comparison.

## 4. Say how sure you are

For each claim, say in plain words how well supported it is: well supported, plausible but thinly supported, contested, or unsupported. Name what would change your view. Keep dates in view: a figure can be accurate for its year and out of date now.

## 5. Conclude only what the evidence justifies

- Answer the owner's question directly, then give the reasons in order of weight.
- Your conclusion goes no further than the strongest support. When the evidence is mixed, the conclusion says it is mixed.
- Separate what the evidence shows from your own judgment, and mark the judgment as yours.

## 6. Report

Keep it short and in the owner's language:

- the answer to the question in one or two sentences;
- the key claims with their support and their weak spots, each with its source link next to it;
- where people disagree, and what is uncertain;
- what you could not check.

Do not present a claim as checked when you only read it in the material.
