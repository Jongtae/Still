Industry hypotheses and counterarguments

This page sets out the whitepaper's hypotheses about where companies can create value when AI supply is separated into layers, and the counterarguments and alternative scenarios it raises against them. It is not a validated market size or a revenue forecast.

Manuscripts: Whitepaper ch. 10–11, Compendium ch. 20–21. Last updated October 14, 2026.

What this page covers

Basis Hypothesis. Whitepaper ch. 10

Chapter 10 of the whitepaper says it deals with business directions drawn from the earlier industry hypotheses, not with a validated market size or revenue forecast. Every table and sentence on this page should be read within that limit. It does not predict which company will win. It looks at how existing businesses' strengths and weaknesses change once customers start choosing things separately.

This structure does not remove existing businesses. Model providers can run integrated products and external supply together, and device, telecom and cloud businesses can find roles in execution environments and operations.

Who pays for a personal agent

Basis Hypothesis. Compendium 20.2

If you propose an agent that acts on the user's side, the revenue model has to be examined alongside that promise. In a user subscription, users must feel recurring benefit; keeping memory and control may not be enough, and actual task completion and recovery have to give a reason to pay. Where a company pays, its work authority must be kept apart from the employee's personal context; paying the bill must never be read as unlimited access to all personal information and activity. Where revenue comes from seller fees or transactions, how commercial interests shape recommendations and actions must be visible.

Who pays is an accounting question and also a product question: it explains on whose behalf the agent acts.

Compendium 20.2

Economics measured on successful work

Basis Proposal. Compendium 20.3

The whitepaper proposes not evaluating the economics of agent services by token cost alone. Count failed attempts, re-runs, maintenance when external services change, customer support, and the user's review and recovery burden, and look at the total cost of delivering one completed result the user accepted. Divide the total delivery cost in an observation period by the number of accepted completions, include resources spent on failures and cancellations, and report the success rate alongside. With zero completions, there is no low unit cost to report. Show the user's review time separately from the business's cash cost, and disclose the assumptions if time is converted to money.

If a professional skill shortens execution but takes more time to check for errors, the benefit is small. Conversely, even without full automation, getting the user's judgment at the right moment and reducing errors and recovery has value. When adding execution evidence, weigh the cost of issuing, transmitting, storing and verifying it against the investigation, dispute and support costs it saves. So the goal is not "as much autonomous action as possible" but a trustworthy completion experience at a cost people can pay.

Note This is an evaluation method the whitepaper proposes, not the result of a profitability calculation. This guide contains no cost or revenue figures either.

A switch that does not need everyone to move at once

Basis Proposal. Compendium 20.4

If value appears only once every service issues standard evidence and every memory moves perfectly, adoption is hard. The whitepaper proposes creating usefulness first within connections already allowed and a limited range of tasks, then expanding by reducing the recurring burden of moving, recovering and collaborating. A personal agent business can start with checking and correcting memory and resuming work; a service business can start with state queries and change confirmation for tasks with proven demand. Exchange of evidence between businesses comes later, within the range where it reduces real investigation and support cost. This is an example of staged value checks, not a development schedule or a required order.

Sellers have reason to weigh the benefit of allowing outside agents against fewer customer touchpoints, integration maintenance and the cost of handling wrong executions. A user-side agent being useful does not by itself give sellers a reason to take part. The test for adoption is not agreement on openness but whether, for each participant, the benefit exceeds the burden of integration, operation and verification.

Counterarguments

Basis Hypothesis. Whitepaper ch. 11, Compendium ch. 21

An integrated product may be better

A product that optimizes model, memory and execution environment together may give a more consistent experience; this is an important counterargument. If a structure that separates the user-side layer complicates setup, adds failure points and lowers quality, theoretical choice does not become real benefit. So separation is not a goal in itself. What has to be verified is whether real replaceability can be offered while keeping the convenience of integration. The compendium reads the decision to vary models only within the main AI's family, and the dropping of Kubernetes, as practical judgments in this direction.

Users may choose convenience over portability

Users finding provider lock-in inconvenient does not mean they will pay for or often use transfer features. Portability may be a latent, insurance-like value and a low priority in everyday purchase decisions. So a product has to be chosen for the value of the work it does now, not just for control.

A personal agent can itself become a new control point

If a personal agent mediates the choice and execution of many services, it can gain influence over which services are discovered, recommended and used. Becoming independent of model providers only to be locked into an agent platform is not the outcome the whitepaper aims at. Service selection criteria, commercial relationships and the history of authority use must be reviewable along with memory and work state. Evidence systems are no exception: if the information and access needed for verification are tied to one business, execution evidence becomes a new lock-in point.

User control does not mean owning external services' authority

Even when a person manages their own memory and delegations, they cannot invent access paths or records an external service does not provide. Account access, data use and a service's processing conditions must be checked for each connection. The banner of a user-led agent is no excuse for bypassing a service's access restrictions or asserting unproven results as facts. When a needed connection or evidence cannot be obtained, present the allowed alternatives, user intervention and what cannot be confirmed. If these limits recur in major tasks and cost more than the benefit of resolving them, the business case for an independent agent layer weakens. That possibility must not be left out while presenting differentiation as the inevitable future.

Not assuming a single future

Basis Hypothesis. Whitepaper 11.4, Compendium 21.4

The whitepaper sees three structures that may compete or coexist.

  • Integrated structure: one provider offers everything from the model to the assistant experience.
  • Connected platform structure: many models and services are connected but a central platform manages core context and distribution.
  • User-led structure: the user keeps control over personal context and delegation while changing providers and execution environments.

These are scenarios for comparison, not predictions with probabilities, and they are not mutually exclusive: an integrated product can also offer user-led transfer and revocation. The point is not to declare that the market must go to the third structure, but to set criteria for what switchability and means of control users must have, whichever structure is chosen.
